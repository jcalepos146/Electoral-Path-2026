Swing: the county's 2026 margin minus its presidential margin. Trend: that swing minus the national shift over the same period (— toward Democrats since 2024, — since 2020). How this isolates the turnout gap

The scenario reverses the four-point 2022 difference, rather than copying the literal 71% and 67% levels into 2026. Let H and T be the sizes of the 2024 Harris and Trump coalitions. Solve H × Harris turnout + T × Trump turnout = the baseline combined number of voters, and Harris turnout − Trump turnout = the selected gap. Rates are percentages of each group. Each state then follows the existing simulator’s turnout model, holding within-group vote choice fixed.

Applying this scenario resets the other simulator controls, including any no-war adjustment. The seat totals are illustrative model outputs; uncalled races use their leaders and Alaska uses the existing ranked-choice approximation. This estimates a turnout-only change, not how much of the polling error turnout caused. The 2025 New Jersey polling comparison and the 2026 J.L. Partners decomposition provide different kinds of evidence.

Calculation and limits

Fit an unweighted least-squares line to approval against field-end date within the selected window. Only polls whose entire fieldwork starts inside the window and ends before the war cutoff enter the fit. Start the hypothetical path at the last prewar observation, then extend zero, half or all of the fitted monthly slope through Nov. 3; approval is bounded to 0–100%.

Hypothetical House margin = counted 2026 D−R margin − sensitivity coefficient × (hypothetical approval − observed October approval). Both approval readings concern all adults. Their difference is applied to the election margin; the voter-only approval slider is not substituted for either reading. Applying this scenario resets the simulator to its default approval and turnout before applying just this national shift, avoiding double counting.

The scenario holds turnout and candidate effects fixed. It does not assign all post-February changes to the war, and it can imply a worse Republican outcome for some trend choices. Uniform shifts across states and districts are illustrative, especially for ranked-choice contests and runoffs. Shifts beyond the simulator’s ±20-point range cannot be applied.

    How the simulator works

    Each race starts at its actual 2026 margin, then gets three adjustments: the national environment shift, the approval change, and the turnout changes for its state.

    Turnout: the 2026 Voter Poll split voters by their 2024 vote. Each group's turnout rate is estimated by dividing its share of the roughly 115 million 2026 House votes counted by Nov. 9 by its 2024 vote total; for people who skipped 2024, the denominator is the eligible population (UF Election Lab) minus 2024 ballots. Each state's electorate is estimated from its own 2024 or 2020 result, scaled so the nation matches the poll. A slider sets the turnout rate nationally, and every state's group moves by the same proportion. Each group then votes the way it did nationally in the poll. With the 2020 baseline, 2020 Biden and Trump voters are assumed to turn out in the same mix and vote like 2024 Harris and Trump voters.

    Approval: each point moves 1% of voters between "somewhat disapprove" and "somewhat approve", using those groups' House vote in the poll. This assumes only soft opinions change.

    House districts use each district's count as of Nov. 9; uncontested districts count as 100-point margins. Turnout effects use the state's electorate. Where an independent was the main challenger to a Republican, the independent is treated as the challenger. With a 50–50 Senate the vice president, a Republican, breaks ties. This is a what-if tool, not a forecast.

    Sources and method

    Race results and calls: Associated Press tallies as compiled on Wikipedia's 2026 House, Senate and governor election pages (original snapshot Nov. 8–9, 2026). Selected House winners rechecked against Wikipedia district tables Nov. 17; Mississippi Senate and Kansas governor calls updated from AP reporting. See the update log above for per-race sources. Vote shares may lag calls. NC-3 remains uncalled despite Murphy’s concession; its simulator margin uses a newer DDHQ count.

    Notes on uncalled races come from AP reporting on the Mississippi count and on Alaska's ranked-choice timetable. "Changed party" compares the winner with the party that held the seat or office before the election; for House seats redrawn mid-decade, it follows the source's gain and hold labels.

    Voter trends: the national House exit-poll figures transcribed from Wikipedia’s 2026 House elections page, labeled there as Edison. These are survey estimates, not counts. The separately labeled J.L. Partners survey includes nonvoters and uses a different denominator; AP-NORC’s approval series covers all adults.

    County map: 2020 and 2024 presidential results by county from the tonmcg/US_County_Level_Election_Results_08-24 dataset; 2026 county results for Senate, governor and House races from civicAPI, unofficial as of Nov. 9. civicAPI doesn't publish where its data comes from; its statewide totals agree with AP-reported results within 1.5 points everywhere, and closer where counting has finished. County outlines from the us-atlas package.

    Ballot measures: counts from civicAPI; descriptions from Wikipedia's 2026 ballot measures page where the measure could be matched. Turnout: votes in each state's largest 2026 statewide count compared with presidential votes summed from county results.

    Simulator: starting margins from civicAPI counts as of Nov. 9 (race calls stay with AP); turnout rates estimated from the 2026 Voter Poll, the roughly 115 million House votes counted by Nov. 9, 2024 and 2020 presidential totals, and the UF Election Lab's 2024 eligible-population and ballots estimates.

    Not included: precinct results, state-by-state demographic estimates, and live updates. This is a fixed snapshot and does not refresh on its own.
